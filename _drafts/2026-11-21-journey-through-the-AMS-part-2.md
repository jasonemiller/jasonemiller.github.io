Within hours of getting home, I resumed researching urologists.  My thoughts kept turning to my neighbor's enthusiasm for UCLA, and I kept checking the Blue Cross web site to verify that UCLA doctors were in-network for me.  There were a few.  I called UCLA.  The person I spoke with suggested that [Dr. Robert E. Reiter](https://www.uclahealth.org/providers/robert-reiter) could see me.  He was available on September 2nd.  His bona fides on his web site looked stellar. 

The Blue Cross web page identified Dr. Reiter as an in-network physician, but that page has screwed me before before. I called Blue Cross to verify with a human that Dr. Reiter was in-network for me.  They said something akin to "it depends."   Insurance is confusing.  Doctors have a license number and a National  Physician ID Number (NPI).  If they are in a practice, their organization has an NPI, too.  Whether or not a doctor is covered depends on these things and, if they are part of an organization like a University hospital, it depends on how the practice bills.  It took conversations with two people at Blue Cross, one person in the urology department at UCLA, and one person in the UCLA Medical Center billing department before I had any faith (not proof, mind you) that consulting with Dr. Reiter would be covered by my insurance.  

I've learned the hard way that proof of insurance is important.  A few months ago, I found a tick on me.  One of my research students is especially wary of ticks and the [diseases they can carry](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CID/DCDC/Pages/Tick-Borne-Diseases.aspx), so I was primed to worry.  Later, online research tuaght me that a tick really has to be attached to you and feeding for quite a while (at least 24 hours), so I hadn't been in any real danger.  But at that moment, my thoughts were to go to Urgent Care with the tick to have them send it to a lab for testing.  I went to the Urgent Care my family had visited in the past (and that the web page showed was in-network[^1]), paid the co-pay, and had a deeply unsatisfying experience with a doctor who really didn't know what to do.  She prescribed antibiotics (that I never picked up) and threw the tick in the trash.  In the next billing cycle, I was informed that the Urgent Care *was not* in-network for me.  I appealed.  My appeal was granted.  But boy, was that at pain.

I learned to verify ahead of time that a medical visit is covered by insurance.  If I got this urology thing wrong, it would be a lot more expensive than going to urgent care. 

[^1]: My wife actually took a screenshot of the Blue Cross web page that showed the Urgent Care to be in-network.  That screenshot probably helped us when we appealed the insurance company's coverage decision.
## Visiting UCLA

The moment my faith in my insurance company exceeded my fear of the American Medical System, I made that September 2nd appointment with Dr. Reiter.  He looked great on paper.  And the meeting met my expectations.

Yes, he said, there are abnormalities on your prostate.  Yes, you should get a biopsy so we know more.  I think he referred to the abnormalities as pyrad 4, saying they merited serious attention.  He then described what the biopsy would be like.  Comparing his biopsy description to what Dr. Poon had told me would only make Dr. Poon look worse, so I won't do it.  But I will say that Dr. Reiter spoke like a scientist physician and he spoke with calm assurance.  He used words like *anterior* and *posteromedial*.  He explained that the biopsy would be [transperineal](https://www.mayoclinic.org/medical-professionals/urology/news/ultrasound-guided-transperineal-prostate-biopsy/mac-20473283) because that gave the best access to the lesions.   "We can reach them more easliy," he said.  Poon had said the biopsy would be transrectal, making it sound like that's "just the way it's done."   I guess this shows one value of getting a second opinion.

My meeting with Dr. Reiter was shorter than I expected.  He was going to set me up with his biopsy team.  "They're great.  They've done thousands of these."  Then he mentioned that I might be a good candidate for [the UCLA BEACON clinical trial](https://www.allclinicaltrials.com/study/NCT07164027).  It's for middle-aged men with my type of lesion.  Would I be interested?  I said I'd be willing to learn more, and he set up a time for me to meet the trial's lead doctor, Dr. Wayne Brisbane.

My meeting with Brisbane was via teleconference a week later.  He was driving his Tesla home from work.  His camera was on the dash, above the steering column.  I could tell me was driving North on the 405.  We had a good conversation about the trial.  I had prepared questions.  Some about risk to me.  Some about how the trial would differ from a normal biopsy.  Some about recovery from the biopsy.  I was able to throw around some ideas and words I'd picked up while I was doing some work in mathematical biology.  His answers were straightforward.  He made me feel like I was asking good questions.

Brisbane said that the trial was intended to study the viability of using a PET scan to replace biopsy in the clinical diagnosis of some prostate abnormalities.  By participating in the trial, I would get a PET scan and an infusion of a radiotagged prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) that is taken up by prostate cancer tissue, illuminating those tissues in the PET scan.  An appointment for the biopsy would follow.  It would be a 'fusion' guided prostate biopsy by two physicians, each blinded to the work and decisions of the other.  One physician would biopsy based on the PSMA PET scan.  The other physician would use the MRI scan.  The second physician would also take a systematic sample of the prostate.  That would conclude my role in the study.  (For those who are counting at home, yes, I would be getting the equivalent of three biopsies!)

When I asked Dr. Brisbane what the risks to me would be, he said, "None, really.  The trial is really designed to give the participants additional benefits.  There's not really any risks beyond those of a regular biopsy."  That risk is a risk of infection.  The trial's biopsy would be transrectal, and I'd receive a strong prophylactic antibiotic before the procedure.  "But we've done thousands of these with no infections."

It all sounded good and safe to me, so I verbally volunteered for the trial before we ended our call.
