On Friday, November 13th, chairs of departments of mathematics, data science, and statistics met together at Fresno State to talk about some important issues facing the mathematics in higher education.  This is a short report on those conversations.  Each section begins with a TL;DR summary followed by a more detailed description of the conversations we had.

## Name Change

The Council changed its name from 'Math Council' to 'MSDS Council' to reflect explicitly the inclusion of Statistics and Data Science in the Council.

## Cal-Bridge

*TL;DR -- [Cal-Bridge](https://calbridge.org) is a great opportunity for CSUCI math/data science/stat students to get support for  the pursuit of a Ph.D.  It requires faculty support.  Cynthia Flores has been our departmental liaison, but there can be more than one person.* 

Cal-Bridge is seeking students who are eager to earn a Ph.D. and be talented in ways that have been historically overlooked by the establishment.  It's a program that began with a focus on physics students, and then it added computer science.  A few years ago, Cal-Bridge added mathematics students.  The last couple cohorts have have about 10 students.

It is a partnership between the California Community Colleges, the CSU, and the UCs that is funded by state appropriations.  Students selected received two years of scholarship support and, when they matriculate to a Ph.D program they get a one-time $40k fellowship.m Because the funds are from the state (and not federal), Cal-Bridge can support AB540 and DACA students.

The next cohort of students will be the class of 2029.  Applications will be due in March/April of 2027. To be eligible, mathematics students have to be interested in something 'mathy' (e.g., have had students go on to earn Ph.D.s in finance, theoretic chemistry, in addition to mathematics.). And students must be motivated to succeeed; they have weekly meetings with mentors, wsork iwht tutors, and participate in workshops with other Cal-Bridge students.

How can we help?  Cal-Bridge needs local mentors at CSU campuses (campus liaisons).  The program needs help getting more applicants,  Menotrs get a modest stipend for a year of work.

## Articulation

*TL;DR - CSU MDSS departments have agreed that in this era of AI, appropriate assessment of student learning in lower division courses must include in-person or proctored assessment, regardless of the modality of the course.  Courses that do not have such assessment should not articulate to a CSU MDSS degree.*

In May 2026, the CSU Math Council passed a [Resolution Regarding Academic Integrity and In-Person Testing Requirements](https://edsource.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/CSU-Resolution-Regarding-Academic-Integrity-FINAL.pdf).  The statement resolves three things:

* that the CSU Mathematics Council recommends that all transferable mathematics and statistics courses include in-person testing as a significant component of the course grade; and

* that the CSU, CCC, and UC systems denote the modality of exams in any official course outline of record; and

*  that the CSU, CCC, and UC systems provide adequate support for students who require in-person supervised proctoring.

This resolution was taken up by the Academic Preparation and Education Programs (APEP) Committee of the CSU Academic Senate, and they submitted [their own version to the body](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1xAzCHt57IB_ieLu1a9umdxV1HlO9DQawbCc3P0xBiEo/edit?tab=t.0).  Their version was submitted for a first reading in front of the body, and the following serious concerns were expressed:

1.  What does this resolution mean equity-wise?  Will it disadvantage students in remote/rural settings?

2. Are the mathematics programs walking their talk?  Do they hold themselves to the same standards?  For example, CSU Online can't require a students to come to a course's campus for an in-person exam.

A rep from APEP at our meeting asked for some clarification and some data from the group, and he indicated that APEP will likely revise their resolution and resubmit to ASCSU in the near future.

Support for the resolution continued to be extraordinarily strong in the room.  All felt that the equity issue at play was not accessibility but was providing students with an authentic learning experience that prepared them for follow-on learning.

Adam Glesser, Chair of the Mathematics Department at CSU Fullerton, shared

>  I wrote [a detailed letter](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1duGMj59F688GTqhBUI5ICXfBk8392zJ-WrmziaR9eQ4/edit?tab=t.0) to my Provost—that includes more recent evidence—after he told our registrar’s office to remove any notations on Assist.org that we had included restricting articulation to courses with in-person proctored assessments. He also told me that I may not deny articulating a course exclusively based on assessment modality. His two main concerns were that, when we deny an articulation based on assessment modality, we are telling those students that they cheated, and also that the CO will reject this because it could lead to bad publicity.

Several times people pointed out that many programs hadn't rolled back accommodations that were put into place to make learning during COVID possible.  Progress in equitable learning requires that we roll back these changes.

The resolution points out the relatity of the system we're operating in and how it needs to change.

Some time was spent talking about the need for state support for testing centers that would allow students to take online courses and have authentic assessment experiences, too.

## Admissions Policy:  Intro to Data Science and Algebra 2

*TL;DR - Introduction to Data Science at the high school level should not count as Algebra 2 in the A-G admissions requirements for the CSU, but CSU appears to be letting it do so.*

In California, US tracks all high school mathematics courses and code them.  Data science courses get a statistics designation.  These codings and designations are managed by the [UC Board of Admissions and Relations with Schools](https://senate.universityofcalifornia.edu/committees/boars/index.html) (BOARS).  Once BOARS determines an admissions requirement, it is law in California.

Three years ago, BOARS decided that no data science course should validate the algebra A_G requirement.  The UC system doesn't let data science count for A-G requirements.  CSU says data sciencce can be taking 'in lieu' (not 'validate') for mathematics, and CSU has not revised its admissions criterion in any substantive way.

Perhaps the Council should pass a resolution and pass it on to ASCSU through APED?

## CTC Single Subject Credential in Math

*TL;DR - The [California Comission on Teacher Credentialling](https://www.ctc.ca.gov) has revised the Subject Matter Requirements for earning a Single Subject Credential in Mathematics.  This change is intentionally lowering the qualifications for math certification in response to political pressure.  Changes to the certification test in mathematics will follow.*

At the meeting, Dr. Juliet Wahleither (CTC Division Director, Research, Evaluation, and Assessment)and Dr. Mary Vixie Sandy (CTC Executive Director) spoke and described how changes to the Single Subject Certification in Mathematics had come about.  Changes flow from AB 130 (in 2021) and AB 126 (in 2026).

The former widened options for demonstrating a student met the single subject requirements.  The latter allowed a student in a `closely related area` to meet the subject requirements as if they were in mathematics.

An area is 'closely related' if it has three required courses in common with those requires of the mathematics major.  This mean, for example, a major that requires precalculus, introductory statistics, and another mathematics course (perhaps Calculus 1) would be classified as 'closely related' to mathematics.

This means that a teacher who earned a degree in a 'closely related area' could get credentialed and be certified to teach AP BC Calculus.

The requirements to get a single-subject certification in mathematics involve five domain requirements that have been revised.  These domain areas reflect the content of the C-SET exam (which hasn't yet been revised in this process), but the CTC's interpretation of how each comain's requirements can be met have been watered down.  Where a domain had once requirement understanding of 'abstract algebra', that is now interpreted as 'algebra'.  Where a domain had once requirement understanding of 'higher geometry', that is now interpreted as 'geometry'.  (These two domains include competencies that can only be satisfied by upper level coursework in mathematics.)  Since algebra and geometry are prerequisites for Calculus 1, a course in Calculus 1 can meet these domain requirements (up to four of the five, in fact).

Calculus is given the exceptional privilege of being able to satisfy multiple domain requirements!  No other course can satisfy multiple domain requirements.

When the CTC staff describe the process they used to arrive at these revisions, the Council was agog at the lack of consultation with the CSU (or any) mathematics community.

The Executive Director made it clear these changes are largely the result of political pressure.  ``We need more mathematics teachers, and not enough people are passing the C-SET exams in mathematics.''

The Council was beside itself with dismay at these changes.

At present, the revisions to the Single Subject Certification requirements in mathematics are beforethe CA Office of Administrative Law.  The changes will also have a public hearing where comments will be heard.  There is still a chance for revisions.  (This happened with the Early Childhood requirements went through a similar process.)

## Chancellor's Office

*TL;DR - We had what felt like a first conversation with the Council's liaison at the CO.*

The Council has a liaison at the Chancellor's Office in David Song-Ruiter, Associate Vice Chancellor for Academic Programs, Innovation, and Faculty Excellence.

We spoke with him about the above issues.

We also spoke with him about ways that the Council could support the new [CSU Formard](https://www.calstate.edu/Strategic-Planning-for-the-CSU) strategic initiative.

One way that the Council could support the strategic plan is to promote the effective use of advisory boards to MSDS departments.  The systewm could leverage its network to provide guidance to departments across the state.

We also asked for CO support for a system-wide mathematics conference; past conferences had been a great success.

Then, we asked the CO to support efforts to allow job seekeers in the mathematical sciences to use [Mathjobs](mathjobs.org) to submit applications for positions in the CSU.  At present, the CSU requires all applications to go through CHRS.  We asked for equitable treatment, noting that Mathjobs is the primary portal for our profession, and lowering a barrier to applying for employment would benefit the CSU.

## AI

*TL;DR - We had a lengthy conversation about how generative AI was affecting our work.  There was enthusiasm about looking at this as a sociological problem solved by doing mathematics in community.  No solutions were identified.*

Some professors are no longer collecting homework.

Some professors assign homework problems and give quizzes over the problems.

Some faculty have taken a "if I don't see you write it, I don't grade it" approach to assessing student work.

At Bakersfield, they are reviewing their RTP policies to accont for AI; one member of the mathematics faculty submitted 20 single-authored papers last year with the assitance of AI, and their AI-generated RTP portfolio was consisted of 700+ folders and subfolders of information that the committee had to sort through.

At East Bay, their Masters comprehensive exam has reverted to paper and pencil (from online).

At Monterey Bay, faculty had a retreat where they examind program and course learning outcomes and how AI would affect them.  Program outcomes didn't change, but they revised many course outcomes.

One person pointed out that the MAA has published a [statement on AI in higher education](https://maa.org/resource/statement-on-ai-in-higher-education/).

Then the conversation turned to framing the impact of AI as a sociological problem, one of needing to convince students to do the work.

Someone referred to [Dave Kung's MAA article](https://maa.org/math-values/less-time-less-learning-lessons-from-research-of-ai-on-student-learning/) on Ai and time-on-task.

San Jose has a Community Room dedicated to having students (and faculty) come hang out and do homework.  It's an enjoyable experience for those who use it, and it gets a lot of use.

The idea of promoting the idea of doing math in community reverberated with the group.
