As soon as I got home from Dr. Poon's, I started looking for a different urologist.  My thoughts kept turning to my neighbor's enthusiasm for UCLA.  UCLA is the most Mayo-like medical organization in my area.  I wanted something like that working for me.

The Blue Cross web site showed that a few UCLA doctors were in-network for me, but the two that my neighbor had recommended weren't taking patients.  

I called the number for UCLA urology.  I asked again about the two doctors my neighbor had mentioned.  The person on the phone said those doctors aren't available, but they suggested that [Dr. Robert E. Reiter](https://www.uclahealth.org/providers/robert-reiter) could see me.  He was available on September 2nd.  His *bona fides* on his web site looked stellar. 

I took the appointment.  Then I started the work of making such my insurance would cover it.

The Blue Cross site identified Dr. Reiter as an in-network physician, but that page has screwed me before before. I called Blue Cross to verify with a human that Dr. Reiter was in-network for me.  They said something akin to "it depends."   Insurance is confusing.  Doctors have a National Physician ID Number (NPI) that insurance uses to identify them.  If a doctor is part of a practice, their practice has an NPI, too.  Whether or not a doctor is covered depends on these things, and it depends on how the practice bills.  It took conversations with two people at Blue Cross, one person in the urology department at UCLA, and one person in the UCLA Medical Center billing department before I had any faith (not proof, mind you) that consulting with Dr. Reiter would be covered by my insurance.  

I've learned the hard way that proof of insurance is important.  A few months ago, I found a tick on me while I was teaching.  One of my research students is especially wary of ticks and the [diseases they can carry](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CID/DCDC/Pages/Tick-Borne-Diseases.aspx), so I was primed to worry about the tick.[^tick]  My thoughts were to go to Urgent Care with the tick and have the doctor send it to a lab for testing.  

I drove straight from work to an urgent care my family had visited in the past (and that the web page showed was in-network[^1]), I paid the co-pay, and I had a deeply unsatisfying experience with a doctor who really didn't know what to do.  She prescribed antibiotics (that I never picked up) and threw the tick in the trash.  In the next billing cycle, I was informed that the urgent care *was not* in-network for me.  I appealed.  A couple months later, my appeal was granted.  But boy, was that at pain.

The experience taught me to verify ahead of time that a medical visit is covered by insurance.  If I got this urology thing wrong, it would be a lot more expensive than going to urgent care. 



## Visiting UCLA

The moment that my insurance research allowed my faith in my insurance company to exceeded my fear of the American Medical System, I made that September 2nd appointment with Dr. Reiter.  He looked great on paper.  And the meeting met my expectations.

The drive to the the main UCLA Medical Center[^2] campus in Westwood took about an hour.  My truck's roof-top box makes parking ramps treacherous, so I found street parking near a Whole Foods and walked to the hospital.

The urology department was easy to find on the first floor, right inside the building's main doors.  The spacious wood paneled waiting room was softer and quieter than the hard tile hallway outside, and the staff at the check-in desk were efficient and kind.  There was a water machine with a stack of paper cups in the corner of the waiting room.

I sat in a wood and fabric chair as I waited with a score of other older men and women.  Am I that old, now?  I didn't feel that old.

Before long, someone came out of a side doorway and called my name.  They ushered me down a back hallway to a small exam room that felt too normal and plain to be at UCLA.  I expected it to be fancier.  But I have little experience with this kind of care.

I think I remember a nurse taking my vitals, and I can't remember if I was then taken to another exam room to meet Dr. Reiter or if he came into mine.  It all seemed to happen quickly and normally.

I remember feeling like Dr. Reiter sorta [resembled his photo](https://www.uclahealth.org/providers/robert-reiter), but he seemed smaller than I'd imagined.  Through our meeting, he sat comfortably on a stool and spoke to me kindly.  First, he asked if a patient advocate could join us.  I said yes and a young woman came into the room, introduced herself, and stood by the far wall, fading into the background.

Yes, he said, there are abnormalities on your prostate.  Yes, you should get a biopsy so we know more.  I think he referred to the abnormalities as "pyrad 4", saying they merited serious attention.  He then described what the biopsy would be like.  Comparing his biopsy description to what Dr. Poon had told me would only only make Dr. Poon look more like a biopsy salesman, so I don't go into detail.  But I will say that Dr. Reiter spoke like a scientist physician and he spoke with calm assurance.  He used words like *anterior* and *posteromedial*.  I let them wash over me.  He explained that the biopsy would be [transperineal](https://www.mayoclinic.org/medical-professionals/urology/news/ultrasound-guided-transperineal-prostate-biopsy/mac-20473283) because that gave the best access to the lesions.   "We can reach them more easliy," he said.  Poon had said the biopsy would be transrectal, making it sound like that's "just the way it's done."   

My meeting with Dr. Reiter was shorter than I expected.  He was going to set me up with his biopsy team.  "They're great.  They've done thousands of these."  Then he mentioned that I might be a good candidate for [the UCLA BEACON clinical trial](https://www.allclinicaltrials.com/study/NCT07164027).  It's for middle-aged men with my type of lesion.  Would I be interested?  I said I'd be willing to learn more, and he set up a time for me to meet the trial's lead doctor, Dr. Wayne Brisbane.

I said I was willing to learn more.  He said he'd set up a meeting.  He then said goodbye, and I left our exam room with the patient advocate.   As she and I walked down the clinic hallway, I said I was driving back to Camarillo.  "Oh," she said, lowering her voice a bit and stepping closer.  "If you like pastries, you just *must* try the new [Carrara Cafe](https://www.carraras.com/locations/camarillo-ca) in Old Town Camarillo."  I thanked her for her recommendation, and we parted.

## The BEACON trial

My meeting with Brisbane was via teleconference a week later.  He was driving his Tesla home from work.  His camera was on the dash, above the steering column.  I could tell he was driving North on the 405.  We had a good conversation about the trial.  I had prepared questions.  Some about risk to me.  Some about how the trial would differ from a normal biopsy.  Some about recovery from the biopsy.  His answers were straightforward.  He made me feel like I was asking good questions.

Dr. Brisbane said that the trial was intended to study the viability of using a [PET](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/pet-scan/about/pac-20385078) scan to replace biopsy in the clinical diagnosis of some prostate abnormalities.  By participating in the trial, I would get a PET scan and an infusion of a radiotagged prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) that is taken up by prostate cancer tissue, illuminating those tissues in the PET scan.  An appointment for the biopsy would follow.  THe biopsy would be a 'fusion' guided prostate biopsy by two physicians, each blinded to the work and decisions of the other.  One physician would biopsy based on the PSMA PET scan.  The other physician would use the MRI scan.  The second physician would also take a systematic sample of the prostate.  That would conclude my role in the study.  (For those who are counting at home, yes, my prostate would be getting hit with the equivalent of about three biopsies!)

When I asked Dr. Brisbane what the risks to me would be, he said, "None, really.  The trial is really designed to give the participants additional benefits.  There's not really any risks beyond those of a regular biopsy."  That risk is a risk of infection.  The trial's biopsy would be transrectal, and I'd receive a strong prophylactic antibiotic before the procedure.  "But we've done thousands of these with no infections."

It all sounded good and safe to me, so I verbally volunteered for the trial before we ended our call.
